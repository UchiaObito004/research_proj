Abstract—Post-harvest fruit spoilage represents a major economic and ecological challenge throughout the global agricultural supply chain. Manual quality inspection is labor-intensive, subjective, prone to human error, and impractical for industrial-scale throughput. In this study, an automated computer vision framework is developed for binary fruit freshness classification (fresh vs. rotten) using deep transfer learning. We conduct an empirical comparative investigation between two convolutional architectures: MobileNetV2 (featuring inverted residual bottlenecks) and EfficientNetB0 (featuring compound scaling). Both networks were augmented with custom classification heads comprising Global Average Pooling, Batch Normalization, and Dropout regularized dense projections, and evaluated on a benchmark dataset of 13,599 images across diverse fruit varieties. Comprehensive empirical evaluation demonstrates that MobileNetV2 decisively outperforms EfficientNetB0, attaining a Test Accuracy of 97.96%, an Area Under the ROC Curve (ROC-AUC) of 0.9985, a Macro F1-Score of 0.9792, and a Pearson prediction correlation of 0.9695 with ground-truth labels. Furthermore, with a compact model footprint of only 2.61 million parameters (13.11 MB), MobileNetV2 provides the optimal balance of high discriminative power and computational efficiency. The champion model is fully deployed into an end-to-end operational pipeline featuring a high-performance FastAPI backend and an interactive Streamlit user dashboard for real-time agricultural quality assessment.
Keywords—Fruit Freshness Detection, Deep Learning, MobileNetV2, EfficientNetB0, Transfer Learning, Computer Vision, Convolutional Neural Networks, Food Quality Assessment, ROC-AUC Analysis, Real-Time Edge Deployment.

I. Introduction

Agricultural produce and fresh fruits are highly perishable commodities subject to rapid physiological deterioration, enzymatic browning, and microbial contamination throughout post-harvest handling, transit, storage, and retail distribution [1]. According to recent reports published by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), approximately one-third of all food manufactured globally for human consumption is lost or wasted annually, with fruit and vegetable losses frequently exceeding 40% to 50% across developing nations [2]. A primary contributor to this substantial waste is the inability of supply chain stakeholders to rapidly, objectively, and non-destructively isolate decaying produce before microbial pathogens and volatile ethylene emissions trigger accelerated cross-contamination throughout storage containers.

Traditional fruit sorting and quality grading methodologies predominantly depend on manual human sensory inspection. However, manual grading suffers from several critical bottlenecks [3]:

  • Subjectivity and Inconsistency: Human visual evaluation varies significantly among inspectors and is severely degraded by physical fatigue, visual adaptation, ambient illumination shifts, and cognitive biases.
  • Throughput Limitations: Manual sorting cannot match the operational velocities required by modern industrial conveyor packing facilities, which handle thousands of units per hour.
  • High Economic Overhead: Continuous labor costs and training requirements make large-scale manual inspection economically unsustainable.

To overcome these challenges, automated optical sorting driven by computer vision and machine learning has emerged as a transformative paradigm. Early computer vision frameworks relied on hand-engineered feature descriptors, such as color histograms, Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrices (GLCM), and Gabor filters paired with classical classifiers [4]. While moderately effective under pristine, uniform laboratory illumination, these methods degrade rapidly when exposed to non-uniform ambient illumination, natural fruit skin pigmentation variations, superficial surface dust, and occlusions [5].

In contrast, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have revolutionized image classification by directly extracting hierarchical spatial representations from raw pixel tensors. Through stacked convolutional, pooling, and non-linear activation layers, CNNs learn multi-scale feature hierarchies—progressing from low-level edges and textures to complex semantic representations of surface bruising, fungal mycelia, and tissue softening [6].

Nevertheless, industrial agricultural environments demand models that achieve not only high diagnostic precision, but also extreme parameter efficiency and low inference latency to enable execution on edge microcontrollers, robotic sorting arms, and mobile inspection devices without requiring high-power data center servers. To address these operational constraints, this paper conducts a rigorous comparative study between two lightweight deep architectures: MobileNetV2 and EfficientNetB0. We systematically evaluate their feature extraction efficacy, generalization ability, convergence dynamics, and deployment feasibility for automated fruit freshness classification.

II. Literature Review

Automated agricultural produce inspection and disease recognition have evolved substantially over the past decade, progressing from hand-crafted statistical learning to advanced deep learning architectures [4]. This section examines existing methodologies, identifies research gaps, and highlights recent developments in computer vision for food quality control.

A. Challenges in Automated Fruit Quality Inspection

Automated fruit freshness grading faces significant biological and optical challenges [5]:

  • Class Boundary Ambiguity: Early-stage fungal infection and internal physiological breakdown often exhibit subtle surface manifestations that closely resemble natural skin pigmentation or harmless cosmetic blemishes.
  • Intra-Class Morphological Variability: Fruits within the same botanical class display extensive variations in shape, size, color ripeness, and surface curvature, making rigid rule-based classification ineffective.
  • Real-Time Processing Constraints: Industrial sorting facilities require classification latencies below 500 ms per item to sustain operational conveyor belt throughput without introducing processing bottlenecks.

B. Recent Advances in Deep Learning for Agri-Food

The application of deep transfer learning has proven instrumental in overcoming dataset constraints in agriculture [7]. By leveraging weights pre-trained on massive computer vision corpuses such as ImageNet-1k, networks transfer generalized visual representations (e.g., edge detectors, gradient orientations, and surface textures) to specialized agricultural domains with minimal retraining overhead. C. Outcome of Literature Analysis

The comparative literature analysis reveals several foundational insights:

  • Superiority of Convolutional Architectures: Across all surveyed studies, deep CNNs consistently surpass traditional machine learning models (e.g., SVM, Random Forest) by 15% to 25% in classification accuracy when handling unconstrained natural lighting.
  • Trade-Off Between Depth and Efficiency: Heavyweight networks such as VGG-16 and ResNet-50 achieve high accuracy but incur memory footprints exceeding 100 MB to 500 MB, rendering them impractical for low-cost embedded sorting hardware.
  • Identified Research Gap: There is a lack of rigorous, standardized head-to-head empirical evaluations directly comparing MobileNetV2 and EfficientNetB0 on large-scale multi-fruit freshness datasets under identical regularization budgets and end-to-end web deployment pipelines. This study directly addresses this gap.

III. Methodology

The proposed framework follows an end-to-end machine learning engineering workflow, comprising: (1) dataset acquisition and curation, (2) automated data augmentation and preprocessing, (3) transfer learning model construction, (4) standardized hyperparameter optimization, and (5) comparative statistical evaluation.

A. Dataset Description

The experimental benchmark is conducted using the public Fruits Fresh and Rotten for Classification dataset curated by Sriram et al., accessible via KaggleHub [16]. The dataset incorporates high-resolution optical imagery of multiple commercially vital fruit varieties, including apples, bananas, and oranges, photographed under varying orientations, backgrounds, and decay stages. The dataset is organized into two mutually exclusive target classes: Fresh (unblemished, mature fruits) and Rotten (fruits exhibiting visible surface rot, mold growth, or physiological softening) as illustrated in Fig. 1.

B. Preprocessing & Data Augmentation

Before network ingestion, all source images undergo standardized preprocessing and stochastic data augmentation to mitigate overfitting and simulate conveyor lighting and camera variability:

  • Spatial Resizing: All source images are bilinearly interpolated to fixed input tensor dimensions of 224 × 224 × 3 pixels to match ImageNet pre-training standards.
  • Channel Normalization: Pixel intensity values are scaled from the integer range [0, 255] to the floating-point range [0.0, 1.0] via multiplicative scaling:
    xnorm = x / 255.0
  • Data Augmentation Regimen: Applied dynamically to training batches:
    • Random rotation up to ±25° to emulate random conveyor fruit orientation.
    • Random zoom magnification between [0.8, 1.2] to account for camera-to-fruit distance variations.
    • Random width and height shifts up to 10% to prevent spatial center bias.
    • Random horizontal reflections (p = 0.5) to enforce bilateral symmetry invariance.
  • Validation Stratification: The training pool is partitioned into 80% training (8,721 images) and 20% validation (2,180 images) subsets using seed = 42. The test set (2,698 images) is strictly held out and evaluated without augmentation.

IV. System Architecture

To benchmark transfer learning efficacy, two pre-trained feature extractors are evaluated under frozen backbone configurations as diagrammed in Fig. 2:

  • MobileNetV2: Employs inverted residual bottlenecks with linear bottleneck layers [17]. A 1×1 expansion convolution projects low-dimensional input manifolds into high-dimensional representations, followed by a 3×3 depthwise separable convolution and a 1×1 projection layer. This drastically reduces multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations while preserving representational expressiveness.
  • EfficientNetB0: Employs neural architecture search to establish an optimal baseline network (MBConv blocks with squeeze-and-excitation optimization), systematically scaled via a compound scaling coefficient that balances network depth, width, and resolution [18]:
    depth: d = αφ,   width: w = βφ,   resolution: r = γφ
    where α · β2 · γ2 ≈ 2 and φ is the user-defined compound coefficient.
Fig. 2. Proposed Deep Transfer Learning Architecture with Custom Regularized Head.

Custom Regularized Classification Head

To map high-dimensional convolutional feature maps to the binary freshness target while preventing overfitting, an identical custom head was appended to both backbones:

  1. Global Average Pooling 2D (GAP): Computes spatial average across feature maps, condensing tensor shape (7, 7, C) into a 1D vector of length C, drastically lowering parameter count compared to flatten layers.
  2. Batch Normalization (BN): Stabilizes internal covariate shift, accelerating training convergence [19]:
    y = γ · (x - μ) / √(σ2 + ε) + β
  3. Dense Layer 1: 256 hidden units with ReLU activation: f(z) = max(0, z).
  4. Dropout 1: Regularization with dropout probability p = 0.40 [20].
  5. Batch Normalization 2: Additional normalization of intermediate representations.
  6. Dense Layer 2: 64 hidden units with ReLU activation.
  7. Dropout 2: Secondary regularization with dropout probability p = 0.20.
  8. Output Layer: Single neuron with Sigmoid activation:
    σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e-z)
    producing predicted probability P(Rotten | Image) ∈ [0, 1].

Training Optimization Regimen

Both networks were trained under identical optimization hyperparameters:

  • Loss Function: Binary Cross-Entropy (Log Loss):
    ℒBCE = -1/N ∑ [yi log(ŷi) + (1 - yi) log(1 - ŷi)]
  • Optimizer: Adam (β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, ε = 10-7) with learning rate η = 10-4.
  • Batch Size: 32 samples per mini-batch.
  • Callbacks: EarlyStopping (patience = 5, restore_best_weights = True), ReduceLROnPlateau (factor = 0.5, patience = 2, min_lr = 10-6), and ModelCheckpoint saving best weights based on validation accuracy.

V. Implementation & Deployment

To demonstrate industrial and real-world applicability, the champion model was integrated into a production-ready dual-tier application stack as diagrammed in Fig. 3:

  • FastAPI Backend Microservice (api.py): A high-throughput asynchronous RESTful API server. Upon receiving a multipart HTTP POST request containing an uploaded fruit image, the server performs RGB conversion, 224×224 resizing, array normalization, and model inference. The endpoint returns a JSON response containing the predicted label, confidence score, and execution latency.
  • Streamlit Frontend Dashboard (app.py): An interactive graphical web dashboard designed for agricultural operators. Users upload fruit images via drag-and-drop, trigger instant analysis, and receive color-coded diagnostic feedback (Green for Fresh, Red for Rotten) with confidence percentages.

VII. Conclusion and Future Scope

The usage of a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) transfer learning architecture for fruit freshness detection is presented in this inquiry as a whole. A wide variety of multi-fruit images across distinct varieties and decay stages were used to train and evaluate the models, ensuring robust and adaptive functioning. We trained the transfer learning models with painstaking attention to detail, and the result was an astounding accuracy of 97.96% for MobileNetV2. The empirical results demonstrate that MobileNetV2 does an outstanding job of identifying freshness states in optical images.

Key findings include:

  • Architectural Efficiency: Inverted residual bottlenecks with depthwise separable convolutions enabled MobileNetV2 to capture localized defect features with only 2.61 million parameters.
  • Generalization Power: Data augmentation techniques, including rotation, zoom, spatial shifts, and horizontal flips, made the model resilient against orientation and illumination variations.
  • Comprehensive Diagnostic Superiority: Confusion matrices, ROC-AUC curves (0.9985), and Pearson correlation (0.9695) established MobileNetV2 as the definitive champion model.

Numerous real-world applications could benefit from the model's high accuracy and low latency. These include automated conveyor belt sorting in packaging houses, supermarket inventory shelf monitoring, and quality grading for wholesale agricultural procurement.

Future Scope: There are significant opportunities for further research and development in fruit quality assessment thanks to the promising results of this study. To build a more comprehensive system that provides a deeper understanding of produce condition, it is vital to combine optical RGB inspection with additional modalities, including Near-Infrared (NIR) hyperspectral imaging for internal browning detection, and acoustic resonance testing for firmness evaluation.

Another important direction is real-time edge acceleration. Quantizing the MobileNetV2 model to 8-bit integers (INT8) via TensorFlow Lite and deploying it onto ultra-low-power edge microcontrollers (such as Raspberry Pi 5, Google Coral Edge TPU, and NVIDIA Jetson Nano) will enable autonomous robotic sorters to operate directly in orchard and packing environments without internet connectivity. Furthermore, expanding the classification taxonomy from binary freshness to multi-class defect diagnosis (e.g., distinguishing fungal rot from mechanical bruising or physiological scald) will provide actionable insights for agricultural waste reduction.
