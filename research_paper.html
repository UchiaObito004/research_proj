Abstract—Harvest fruit is highly susceptible to food losses and wastage in food supply chains due to its perishability before reaching the consumers. The manual human inspection is slow, subjective, tiresome and cannot follow the sorting speeds used in commercial packing lines. To tackle this issue with automation, we consider automated binary freshness sorting (fresh vs. rotten) based on deep transfer learning, with two lightweight models: MobileNetV2, an inverted residual bottleneck model, and EfficientNetB0, a compound scaling model. They are built with the same custom classification head regularized using 3 layers: Global Average Pooling, Batch Normalization and Dropout, and trained and tested on a standard benchmark multi-fruit image corpus, which has 13,599 images of apples, bananas and oranges. Experimental results were extremely competitive: MobileNetV2's test accuracy was 99.41% (improving by 1.04% over the baseline), its ROC-AUC was 0.9985, macro F1-score was 0.9792, and the Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.9695 between MobileNetV2's predictions and ground-truth labels. When using the frozen feature extraction, EfficientNetB0 did not give any consistent solution (49.89% accuracy), while MobileNetV2 was converging successfully. In addition to classification accuracy, MobileNetV2 has very low resource consumption, as it has only 2.61 million parameters and a disk space of 13.11 MB, which is ideal for edge deployments. The final model was integrated into an end-to-end high throughput pipeline with FastAPI backend and interactive Streamlit graphical dashboard for real-time agricultural quality assessment.
Keywords—Fruit freshness detection, MobileNetV2, EfficientNetB0, transfer learning, computer vision, food quality assessment, convolutional neural networks, edge deployment, ROC-AUC analysis.

I. INTRODUCTION

As soon as picked, fresh fruits start to spoil. In all packing, washing, transport and stocking areas, tissues softening, enzymatic browning and fungal decay are easily induced on produce. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) estimates that 67% of all food produced for human consumption is lost or wasted during transport and handling, and the losses in developing countries are as high as 40% to 50% for fruit and vegetable products [2]. Much of this waste is attributed to the absence of rapid, non-destructive diagnostic tools in the distribution center to minimize produce decay and air borne fungal cross contamination as a result of volatilized ethylene emission within the storage containers.

Most packhouses still use manual sorters located on conveyor belts. This manual inspection process has been well established as having disadvantages in practice [3]:

  • Subjectivity and Inconsistency: Human visual evaluations change over long shifts as they get physically tired, adapted to the light, and vary in their opinion of what is considered an acceptable blemish on skin. High operational speeds are required by today's industrial conveyor packing house and manual sorters are ineffective to meet these high speeds because thousands of fruit units have to be sorted in a single hour.
  • High Operating Costs: Large scale manual inspection is not economically viable for commercial packaging companies due to the regular labor costs and ongoing training needs.

Machine vision is a practical solution on these operational problems, a reliable alternative to manual inspection. Handmade statistical feature descriptors including color histograms, Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrices (GLCM), Gabor wavelets and classical classifiers such as Naive Bayes, Maximum Likelihood, neural networks with fuzzy rules, and support vector machines (SVM) [4] were widely used in early automated pipelines. These methods are capable of achieving reasonably good performance in the controlled laboratory settings, but have difficulty coping with unconstrained lighting, ambient shadows, natural surface mottling, superficial dust and orientation changes [5].

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) address this by learning the visual discriminations directly from the raw pixel matrix. The surface features, from basic edge boundaries to more intricate skin structures that show mycelial growth, bruising and necrosis, are naturally separated by hierarchical convolutional layers [6].

But in a practical implementation in a sorting belt, there are very strict limitations. For on-farm and packhouse sorting, models must be parameter efficient, have low inference latency, and be extremely accurate for the sorting to be feasible at the edge, on microcontrollers or robotic sorting arms, instead of on data center servers. In this regard, this paper has been written to compare the runtime performance of two empirical lightweight deep architectures: MobileNetV2 and EfficientNetB0, which are both expected to address the operational constraint. They are then assessed systematically for their feature extraction and generalization performance, their convergence dynamics and their feasibility to be deployed in automated machine classification of fruit freshness.

II. LITERATURE REVIEW

In the last ten years in particular, automated inspection and defect recognition of produce has come a long way from basic statistical machine learning techniques to a more sophisticated deep convolutional architecture [4]. This section reviews the existing methodologies, outlines the gap in the current research and introduces the latest developments in computer vision technology in the field of food quality control.

A. Problems in Automation of Fruit Quality Inspection

The fruit freshness inspection, which is an automated system, is a challenging task due to the technological barriers like fruit elasticity, fruit quality, fruit color gradability, and biological differences [5].

  • Class Boundary Ambiguity: In early stages of fungal infection, and signs of surface physiology, usually show symptoms of fungal infection and signs of internal physiology. Very subtle physical clues that are very close to the normal skin colour and non-threatening cosmetic blemishes.
  • Intra-Class Morphological Variability: Fruits of the same plant and botanical species vary widely in shape, size, color ripeness and surface curvature and thus a rigidly based classification cannot be achieved.
  • Real-Time Processing Constraints: Industrial sorting facilities have strict real-time operational constraints, such as classification latency of less than 500ms per item, to keep conveyor throughput without reducing the speed of the processing lines.

B. Recent advances in deep learning for agri-food businesses.

In agriculture, deep transfer learning has been found to be very useful in dealing with the problem of data scarcity [7]. Networks are trained on large-scale computer vision data (e.g., ImageNet-1k) and then fine-tuned for the agricultural task to learn specific representations at minimal retraining cost (e.g., edge detector, gradient orientation, surface texture). From the comparative literature analysis, the following basic insights are uncovered:

  • Empirical Advantage of Deep CNNs: Across the surveyed domains, deep CNNs have consistently been superior to traditional machine learning approaches. Deep CNNs have been consistently superior to traditional machine learning approaches across the surveyed domains. It has been shown that under unconstrained natural lighting, it is able to improve the accuracy of learning models (SVM and Random Forest) by 15% to 25%.
  • Accuracy vs. Computational Footprint: Highly accurate large-scale networks such as VGG-16 or ResNet-50 require too much memory (100 MB to 500 MB) and thus are not suitable for low-cost embedded sorting hardware.
  • Specific Benchmarking Need: MobileNetV2 and EfficientNetB0 have not been tested on large-scale multi-fruit datasets (with real field data) with the same budget on regularization and end-to-end web deployment pipelines. This study directly aims to fill this gap.

III. METHODOLOGY

The proposed framework is an end-to-end machine learning engineering workflow that tackles this challenge through: (1) dataset acquisition and curation, (2) automated data augmentation and preprocessing, (3) standardized transfer learning model construction, (4) hyperparameter optimization, and (5) comparative statistical evaluation.

A. Dataset Description

The experiment is done on the public fruits fresh and rotten for classification dataset provided by Sriram et al. [16] on kaggle hub. The collection includes high resolution optical image data of a number of commercially important fruits under different conditions ranging from fruit type (apples, bananas, oranges), orientation, background and fruit ripeness or decay. The data has been divided into two mutually exclusive target classes, namely, the class of fruits that are considered fresh (unblemished and mature fruits are classified into the marketable class) and the class of fruits that are considered rotten (fruits with visible surface rot, mold growth, or physiological softening are classified into the rotten class).

B. Preprocessing & Data Augmentation

Before being ingested into the network, all source images are standardized. Standardised pre-processing and stochastic data augmentation techniques were used to simulate conveyor lighting and camera variations to avoid overfitting:

  • Spatial Resizing: To conform to ImageNet pre-training requirements, all source images are bilinearly resized to input image tensors of fixed size (224 × 224 × 3).
  • Channel Normalization: Pixel intensity values are scaled using multiplicative scaling to fit floating-point numbers in [0.0, 1.0] such that integer values in [0, 255] are normalized to floating-point values in [0.0, 1.0] using the formula:
    xnorm = x / 255.0
  • Data Augmentation Regimen: Dynamically applied to training batches:
    • Randomly rotates fruit by up to 25°, simulating the random orientation of conveyor fruit.
    • To offset random differences in distance between camera and fruit, zoom in and out between 0.8 and 1.2 times.
    • Some random variation in width and height (up to 10%) to avoid spatial center bias.
    • Random horizontal reflection of the image with probability p = 0.5 to enforce bilateral symmetry invariance.
  • Validation Stratification: Using random seed = 42, 80% of the training population (8,721 images) is separated from 20% of the population (2,180 images) for validation. The test set (2,698 images) is kept completely separate and is tested without any enhancement.

IV. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

In order to test the effectiveness of transfer learning, two pre-trained feature extractors are tested in the frozen backbone setting shown in Fig. 2:

  • MobileNetV2: Uses inverted residual bottlenecks and a linear bottleneck layer [17]. The model contains a 1×1 expansion convolution that expands low dimensional input manifolds to higher dimensional representations, a 3×3 depthwise separable convolution, and a linear projection layer of size 1×1. Without loss of representational expressiveness, multiply-accumulate operations are greatly reduced.
  • EfficientNetB0: This baseline network was discovered using neural architecture search (NAS) and is a modified version of MBConv with squeeze and excitation optimization. The dimensions are scaled systematically with a compound scaling coefficient, which is a balance between network depth, width and resolution [18]:
    depth: d = αφ,   width: w = βφ,   resolution: r = γφ
    where α · β2 · γ2 ≈ 2 and φ is a user-defined compound coefficient.
Fig. 2. Proposed Deep Transfer Learning Architecture with Custom Regularized Head.

Custom Regularized Classification Head

In order to map high dimensional convolutional feature maps to the binary freshness target without overfitting, an identical custom head was added to both backbones:

  1. Global Average Pooling 2D (GAP): Computes the spatial mean across feature maps, transforming tensor shape (7, 7, C) into a 1D vector of length C, drastically reducing parameters compared to flatten layers.
  2. Batch Normalization (BN): Normalizes activations to mitigate internal covariate shift, accelerating training convergence [19]:
    y = γ · (x - μ) / √(σ2 + ε) + β
  3. Dense Layer 1: 256 hidden units with ReLU activation: f(z) = max(0, z).
  4. Dropout 1: Regularization with dropout probability p = 0.40 [20].
  5. Batch Normalization 2: Additional normalization of intermediate representations.
  6. Dense Layer 2: 64 hidden units with ReLU activation.
  7. Dropout 2: Secondary regularization with dropout probability p = 0.20.
  8. Output Layer: Single neuron with Sigmoid activation:
    σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e-z)
    generating the predicted probability P(Rotten | Image) ∈ [0, 1].

Training Optimization Regimen

Both networks were optimized under identical hyperparameters:

  • Loss Function: Binary Cross-Entropy (Log Loss):
    ℒBCE = -1/N ∑ [yi log(ŷi) + (1 - yi) log(1 - ŷi)]
  • Optimizer: Adam (β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, ε = 10-7) with learning rate η = 10-4.
  • Batch Size: 32 samples per mini-batch.
  • Callbacks: EarlyStopping (patience = 5, restore_best_weights = True), ReduceLROnPlateau (factor = 0.5, patience = 2, min_lr = 10-6), and ModelCheckpoint saving best weights based on validation accuracy.

V. IMPLEMENTATION & DEPLOYMENT

The trained MobileNetV2 network was incorporated into a multi-tier system ready for industrial and real-world application as shown in Fig. 3:

  • FastAPI Backend Microservice (api.py): A high throughput, asynchronous RESTful API server. The Server would then take the uploaded fruit image in a multipart HTTP POST, convert it into RGB image, resize it to 224×224, normalize the array and apply model inference. The endpoint returns a JSON return with the predicted label, confidence score and execution latency.
  • Streamlit Frontend Dashboard (app.py): A user-friendly and interactive graphical web dashboard for the agricultural operators. Users drag and drop images of fruit and get instant analysis results, with colour-coded diagnostic results (Green: Fresh, Red: Rotten) with confidence percentages.

E. Qualitative Visual Predictions

Examples of qualitative inferences predicted by both candidate architectures on held-out test instances with a variety of fruit types and plant growth stages are illustrated in Fig. 8. Ground truth labels, predicted classes, softmax confidence scores of the model are compared to highlight the differences in their behavior under visual variations found in the real world.

With pristine produce (top row), MobileNetV2 achieves near-deterministic confidence scores of 100.0% for green apples, 99.8% for bananas, and 99.1% for red apples, showing an ability to maintain high invariance under non-uniform lighting, reflections from surface curvature and natural variations in pigmentation. EfficientNetB0 also performs well on the classification of fresh fruits, however with very low confidence scores (56.0%–56.5%), indicating low feature discriminability.

It is clear, performance is considerably greater in the degraded produce (bottom row). MobileNetV2 has a high accuracy of over 99.2% in detecting pathological symptoms for all types of rot, though EfficientNetB0 fails to detect symptoms at a crucial point of misclassification for apple blight (82.3% Fresh) and orange surface mold (74.8% Fresh). This diagnostic error is due to the diffuse receptive field averaging of EfficientNetB0 and its lack of sensitivity to recognizing localized fungal hyphae and necrotic lesions. In contrast, the inverted residual bottlenecks in MobileNetV2 are able to efficiently encode higher frequency edge textures and granular blemishes, thus making it more robust to diagnose for automated industrial sorting.

VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE SCOPE

The present investigation was comparative to reveal the practical issues that are faced with respect to the scale of the parameters, the representation of features, and the ability to make precise diagnostics when using deep transfer learning backbones for automated assessment of fruit freshness after harvest. When fine-tuned under frozen backbone conditions, the evaluation of MobileNetV2 and EfficientNetB0 with multi-fruit images (13,599 images) revealed clear differences in the way the networks operate. The test accuracy reached to 97.96% and the ROC-AUC was 0.9985 with balanced recall in the fresh and rotten categories for MobileNetV2 while EfficientNetB0 failed to converge to similar regularization budgets.

Primary conclusions include:

  • Architectural Efficiency: MobileNetV2 achieved 40.72% reduction in the number of parameters compared to EfficientNetB0 by using Inverted residual bottlenecks and depthwise separable convolutions with a footprint of 13.11 MB using only 2.61 million parameters.
  • Generalization Power: The training was robustly resilient to ambient lighting variation, fruit orientation, and surface reflections, with the addition of generalization Power via dynamic data augmentation, such as random rotations, zoom scaling, spatial shifts and horizontal reflections.
  • Diagnostic Superiority: MobileNetV2 is well-equipped for industrial sorting, showing high ROC-AUC (0.9985) and Pearson correlation (r = 0.9695), while significantly mitigating false-positive detections from superficial blemishes on the skin and eliminating early detection of fungal decay and necrotic lesions.

The empirical results directly contribute to the integration of the technology to be applied within automated conveyor sorting activities in packhouses, inventory management in supermarket distribution centers and intake grading in wholesale agricultural markets.

Future Scope: External rot can be detected with optical RGB inspection with a high degree of reliability, but internal physiological disorders cannot be seen through imaging. For future extensions, RGB sorting will be linked to Near-Infrared (NIR) hyperspectral imaging for non-destructive internal quality evaluation, as well as multimodal sensory fusion for firmness evaluation of fruits [15].

The other logical approach is post training optimization. Future work plans will involve quantizing MobileNetV2 to 8-bit (INT8) integers for ultra-low-power microcontrollers, like the Raspberry Pi 5, Google Coral Edge TPU, and NVIDIA Jetson Nano [10]. This will allow to work with independent robotic sorters working directly in orchard and packing shed without cloud latency and dependence on external networks. Ultimately, the diagnostic pipeline (binary sorting to multi-class defect classification) will provide granular insights to minimise losses and avoid spoilage after harvest.
